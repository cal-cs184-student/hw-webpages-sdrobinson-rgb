Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

In Part 2, I implemented the actual simulation of the cloth mesh. To do so, I implemented the function Cloth::simulate, which re-calculates the positions of the point masses every frame. For basic cloth simulation, simulate first sums the forces acting on a point mass as the sum of all external forces and all spring forces. It then uses this total force along with Verlet integration to calculate and update the position of point mass. My implementation of simulate uses a for loop to do this across all point masses in the Cloth's point_mass vector. Point masses connected by springs also cannot move too far away from each other, constrained by 10% more than the rest length of the connecting spring.

A simulated cloth has many attributes that contribute to the way it behaves. Below are the effects of editing some of them.

pinned2.json with various ks values and normal shading.

ks is the spring constant, which is used when calculating the force a spring is exerting on a point mass. When ks is low, this means the springs exert less force, causing the cloth to droop more when simulated. On the other hand, a high ks results in stronger springs, so the cloth will behave more stiffly and droop less. These effects can be seen in the pictures above. Altering ks had little effect on how the cloth behaved when falling into its final resting position, except some minor differences in intermediate positions that reflected the final positions shown above.

pinned2.json with various density values and normal shading.

Changing the density of a cloth impacts the mass (weight) of the point masses that make it up. Thus, decreasing the density will cause gravity to have less of an effect on the cloth, resulting in the first image above. In the reverse direction, increasing the density causes gravity to have a higher effect, which leads to the droopier cloth shown in the third image above. Interestingly, altering density has a somewhat opposite effect to altering ks. This makes sense, though, as these two values contribute to the forces pulling the cloth down and holding the cloth up respectively, so decreasing one will have a similar effect as increasing the other. Much like with altering ks, the differences in density had minimal effects on the cloth's behavior as it got to the final resting points shown.

pinned2.json with different damping values and normal shading.

damping is the small constant used to slow down a cloth's velocity over time. This is how the simulation accounts for friction and other non-conservative forces. Conversely to the prior two variables we explored, altering the damping value of a simulation has little to no effect on the eventual final resting point of the cloth, instead altering only the path it takes to get there. The exception to this is that a cloth with zero damping could potentially move forever, resulting in no final position. Decreasing the damping value causes the cloth to conserve more speed, so it will swing back and forth multiple times before coming to rest. On the other hand, a cloth with high damping will lose a lot of its speed in every frame, causing it to 'float' down to its final position. Both of these effects can be observed in the screenshots above.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Part 3 of this homework was to give the cloth simulator the ability to simulate collisions with sphere and plane objects. In order to accomplish this, I had to implement a collide function for both Sphere and Plane objects, and call these functions in the simulate function of the cloth. Both collide functions take a point mass by reference, allowing them to alter its position if a collision is detected.

The first collision function I implemented was for the Sphere collision object. To determine if a collision has occurred, my implementation compares the distance between the current position of the point mass and the sphere's origin with the radius of the sphere. If the distance between the two points is less than the sphere's radius, this indicates a collision and the position of the point mass must be 'bumped' up to be just above the sphere. The point mass's tangent point with the sphere is given by where the point mass would hit the surface of the sphere if it travelled away from the origin in the direction of the path connecting the point's 'position' and the sphere's origin. The function then computes a correction vector as the correction that would need to be applied to the point mass's stored last_position to get to this tangent point. The new position of the point mass with the sphere collision handled is the last_position plus this correction vector scaled down by friction.

For collisions with planes, a collision occurs when a point mass's position is on the opposite side of the plane as its last_position. I can mathematically compute this by comparing the dot products of these two position vectors and the normal vector of the planes. If the signs of these two dot products don't match, it means the two positions are on opposite sides of the plane and a collision has occurred. To handle a plane collision, I computed the tangent point using the 't' value given by the equation for ray intersection with a plane (where the 'ray' is defined by an origin at the point mass's last_position and the direction is the direction between the last_position and the position). I plugged this t-value into my fake ray to get the intersection point. My implementation then pushes this point up off the plane by a small amount given by the provided variable SURFACE_OFFSET and computes the correction vector that would need to be added to last_position to get to this point. The point mass's new position is the last_position plus this correction vector scaled down by friction.

The images below show my demonstrate my collision handling implementations in action. In addition to this, the below set of images shows what a cloth draped over a sphere looks like with various values of ks. The results here mirror our experimentation with ks in the previous part, with the additional observation that when ks is low and the spring constraints exert less force on the cloth, the silhouette of the sphere becomes much more pronounced, and increasing ks has the opposite effect. This can be explained by the fact that the collision handling with the sphere object will constrain the point masses making up the cloth for any point masses that want to fall through the sphere. A cloth where the springs have less of an effect on the forces pulling the point masses up will be constrained more heavily by the sphere collision handling, which will cause it to mirror the appearance of the sphere. In the other direction, increasing ks will obscure the sphere, as the more the spring forces pull the cloth up, the less point masses will find themselves attempting to collide with the sphere.
